Describing the Space: Objects

The building splits over two levels and works in zones, not one open room. Entering on the upper level, the cardio machines sit directly in front of the doors. Two food stands share that floor. Off to one side is a turf area with medicine balls, resistance bands and pull-up bars.

Down the stairs is everything else: the weight machines, the basketball and racket courts, the lap pool and the rock climbing wall. The free weights and squat racks are at the very back, which is where I spend nearly all my time.

It is dense with equipment but not cluttered. Walking lanes stay clear. The decoration is the opposite of the equipment — almost none. Where the walls are not glass, they are mirror.

1

Sound

Early morning, when I normally go, it is quiet. Treadmill belts, a plate being set down, short exchanges between two people.

Tuesday around 10:45 in the morning is the one time I cannot go early, and it is packed and loud, with constant movement and overlapping conversation.

I train with both AirPods in, so the sound does not reach me directly. I read the room by watching movement instead.

The same building is two different places depending on the hour. In the morning people come to finish a task and leave. By late morning it is a social destination.

2

What the Space Promotes and Shuts Down

It promotes lifting, cardio, court sports, swimming, climbing, socialising and buying food. When I am in there I feel motivated to train and almost nothing else.

It shuts down sitting still. There is not a single table or chair on the workout floor, so working or reading is not physically possible.

It also shuts down keeping your things with you. Student workers enforce a rule that bags and loose items go in a locker first.

3

Design: Colours, Shapes and Signs

Large windows run along the exterior. A lot of natural light, and a view outside that I use between sets instead of my phone.

Mirrors cover most of the interior wall surface.

There is very little signage: health posters, wipe your machine down, re-rack your weights.

Almost no UCF colour. The one exception is a single wall in the free weight area with a gold stripe and a black stripe painted on grey cinderblock. No logo, no lettering.

Because the walls are either window or mirror, there is barely room for messaging. What survived is what the staff decided they could not leave unsaid.

4

Feeling, Memory, and the People

It feels like a commercial gym, because the layout is the same as any large chain gym. That familiarity works in my favour, since I already know how to read the room.

I keep a squat rack in my garage at home, so the free weight area is a bigger version of a space I already own. Standing in it reminds me of the progress I have made over several years, and the mirrors make that argument without a word.

The people are every experience level at once: beginners, longtime lifters, older members. Nobody is sorted into a section by how much they know. Asking a stranger to work in is normal, and it usually turns into a short conversation.

5

Rhetors and Audiences

Rhetors: UCF administration, who commissioned the building and set its policies. Architects, who chose the two-level split, the glass front, and putting the least intimidating equipment in the sightline of the door. Staff and student workers, who enforce the rules and post the signs.

Equipment manufacturers are rhetors too, since their weight stacks carry printed numbers that turn effort into a readable score. So are the other users: every person who re-racks a plate or leaves one out tells the next person what the norms really are.

Non-human rhetors: the mirrors point you at yourself, the windows point you outside, and the missing tables tell you not to settle in.

Audiences: students who lift, students who use the pool or courts or climbing wall, students passing through for food, the student workers, and the people outside who can see in through the glass.

6

Exigence and Motivations

The exigence is the need behind the building, not what any one person wants out of it, and there is more than one. A university with tens of thousands of students, many without a private gym, has to provide somewhere to be physically active. Academic stress needs a release valve, because student health affects whether students stay enrolled. A large campus needs shared space where students meet outside class. And a modern glass recreation centre gets shown to prospective students.

Motivations sit closer to the ground. The locker rule is safety and liability. No furniture keeps the floor moving. Wipe-down signs are hygiene. Re-racking signs are equipment life and injury prevention. The glass exterior is visibility and pride. The mirrors are form correction and motivation. Beginner-friendly equipment near the door lets a nervous first-timer start without walking through the most intimidating part of the building.

7

Belonging, and Its Limits

What helps: the layout copies the commercial gym template, so I already know how to behave. The free weight area reads as an extension of my garage rack. The mirrors and weight stacks record progress. Asking a stranger to work in is normal. Morning quiet and natural light make it where I clear my head.

What hinders: my belonging is conditional on my purpose, because with no tables or chairs I belong only while I am training. The locker rule makes me surrender my belongings first. The Tuesday crowd turns racks that are mine at seven into racks I have to negotiate for at 10:45. And my AirPods shut out the building’s social energy, which trades some belonging away on purpose.

8

Field Notes: Two Encounters

Huy. I was on the dip machine when another Asian student came over. We exchanged names. His name is Huy, he is from Vietnam, and he is studying electrical engineering. I am an electrical engineering major, so the conversation moved past small talk immediately. I asked whether the courses were hard and we talked through my whole time at that machine. When I finished, I asked for his number. Walking out, my thought was that he would make a good mentor because he is doing the same degree.

We had identified with each other before either of us said anything about engineering: same rough age, same part of the building, same kind of work, and both visibly Asian in a gym where that is not the default.

9

Field Notes: The 10:45 Tuesday

I went in for chest, back and shoulders. After my first exercise the plan stopped working, because the next machine I needed was occupied and so was the one after that. I spent the session walking the floor looking for whatever was open and reordering my workout around other people’s.

I run on a schedule, so improvising annoys me. I did two exercises per muscle group instead of three and left earlier than usual. Nothing about the building had changed since the morning before. The only thing that changed was how many people were in it.

10

The Downs Passage

Downs describes a rhetorical ecology as “a network of myriad interconnecting and almost inseparable elements” (466).

The word that matters is inseparable. No single feature of the RWC produces how I feel in it. The mirrors motivate me because they sit beside free weights that echo my garage, in the quiet of a morning, under natural light, among strangers who will share equipment with me.

Change one element and the meaning changes even though nothing physical has moved. That is exactly what happens at 10:45 on a Tuesday.

11

Terms I Can Use

Nonverbal communication (461) — gym clothes, body language, my AirPods, how Huy and I read each other. Bodies matter as much as minds (463) — the whole space is physical. Rhetoric is always motivated (464). Ecology (466). Rhetors are a network (466).

Exigence (467). Kairos (467) — the 10:45 Tuesday. Narrative (468) — the weight stack tells a story in one number. Presence (469) — the mirrors make progress visible now. Mythos (471) — re-rack your weights. Value (472). Identification and ethos (475) — why Huy and I connected before we spoke.
